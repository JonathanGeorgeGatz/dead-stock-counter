THE DEAD STOCK COUNTER — the exact prompts from the episode, word for word.
Paste them into your AI assistant in order. Prompt 1 builds the first version; prompts 2 and 3
are the fixes from the episode — they exist because the first two versions were wrong in ways
worth understanding before you trust any tool with your shelf.

================================ PROMPT 1 ================================

Build me a single HTML file I can open in my browser, no internet, no account, that tells me
which of my products have stopped selling and how much money is sitting in them.

What it's for: I run a small retail shop. I can export my inventory (SKU, item, category,
units on hand, unit cost, unit price) and my sales (date, SKU, quantity). I want to know what
on my shelves is dead weight, and what it's worth.

Data in: two pastes.
1) The inventory: CSV with columns SKU, Item, Category, On Hand, Unit Cost, Unit Price.
2) The sales: CSV with columns Date, SKU, Qty.

What I want out: the products that aren't selling, how long each has been quiet, what the pile
is worth, and the share of my shelf that's stuck. Show the numbers per product, worst first —
I want a list I can walk the shop with, not just a total.

================================ PROMPT 2 ================================

The first version has the frame wrong, three ways.

- It values my stock at RETAIL price and calls that "cash tied up." The cash I buried in a
  shelf is what I PAID for it. Value everything at unit cost; keep retail only for deciding
  what a markdown would recover.
- It starts from the sales file, so a product that has NEVER sold a single unit — the deadest
  stock I own — never appears anywhere. Start from the INVENTORY file. Every SKU I hold gets a
  verdict: DEAD, SLOW, HEALTHY, or OUT (sold out, zero on hand — different problem, count it
  but leave it out of the money).
- Its idea of pace is a lifetime average over the whole file, so a product that sold hard in
  spring still looks alive in November. Measure pace in the LAST 90 DAYS only: units sold in
  the window, and months of cover = on hand divided by that pace. DEAD means no sale in 90
  days. SLOW means it sells, but the shelf holds six-plus months of it at the current pace.
- "Today" is whatever the newest date in my sales file is — the page must not have a clock of
  its own, or the same files would give different answers on different days.

================================ PROMPT 3 ================================

Now make it trustworthy enough to decide a markdown with.

- All money arithmetic in whole integer cents with one written-down rounding rule (round half
  up). Dates become integer day numbers through one written-down calendar routine — real month
  lengths, real leap years — never a language date parser near the math.
- RETURNS, defined exactly: a negative quantity is a return. A return is NOT a sale — it never
  refreshes a product's last-sale date — and it SUBTRACTS from the 90-day pace. A product
  whose only recent activity is a return is still dead.
- Refuse what you can't read, out loud: a date the calendar can't produce, a quantity that
  isn't a whole number, a duplicate SKU, a sales row whose SKU isn't in my inventory file.
  Each refusal printed with its row and the reason. Never guess, never silently skip.
- Four settings, because those are four business judgments, not constants: how many quiet days
  is dead; how many months of cover is slow; my carrying-cost rate per year; the markdown to
  price a clear-out with. Each one must visibly change the page.
- The page audits itself on every run: inventory rows in = dead + slow + healthy + out +
  refused; total cash summed two independent ways and compared to the cent; sales rows in =
  used + refused; every product's 90-day pace recomputed by a second route and compared. If an
  audit fails, or more than ten percent of either file is refused, print DO NOT TRUST THIS
  COUNT across the top and show nothing else — no tiles, no lists, no numbers to misread.

=========================== THE SECOND OPINION ===========================

The episode also used an independent checker — the same rules implemented again in Python by a
separate session, sharing no code with the page — run on the same two files. checker.py is in
this folder. That second opinion is what caught the return that made dead stock look alive,
the returns that never subtracted from the pace, and the sales rows that vanished without a
count. When two implementations disagree, one of them is wrong, and it is worth finding out
which before you mark anything down.
